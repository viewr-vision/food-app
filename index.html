      Recursive self-improvement for physical AI

      Published
      October 2026
      Author
      Deepanshu Rohilla
      Type
      vision memo · 2 min read

      Everything that has been digitised has been learned. Text, code, images, speech: anything that fits on a screen now has a model that gets better at it every few months.

      But the digital world is a thin film over the physical one, and by our estimate it captures less than 0.001% of it. Under the film are the atoms. Every object that has to be lifted, turned, opened, stacked or carried is still moved by a hand.

      If you can build something that improves itself in the physical world, you unlock realities humans can't yet imagine. It starts with a world where you don't have to lift a finger to move anything, and it ends with every atom around you being manipulated at will.

      Every leap forward has come from learning beyond the limits of reality

      1. 1969
        The limitApollo landing procedures had to account for uncertain computer alarms. One wrong abort call could end the mission.
        The world built to learn inSimulated lunar landings that rehearsed alarms and abort decisions
        What it unlockedApollo 11 flew through the real 1201 and 1202 alarms and landed on the Moon
      2. 2017
        The limitGo AI learned from expert human games. Human knowledge capped where discovery could start.
        The world built to learn inAlphaGo Zero played millions of games against itself
        What it unlockedSuperhuman play with no human game data
      3. 2019
        The limitRobot dexterity depended on real-world experiments. Physical data was slow and expensive.
        The world built to learn inA robotic hand trained in simulations that grew more randomized as it improved
        What it unlockedA real robot hand solved a Rubik's Cube

      Discovery-0

      Published
      October 21, 2026
      Authors
      Argon Robotics team
      Email
      pranay@argonrobotics.ai
      Video
      dishwasher closing · recursive learning

      Discovery-0 is our first demonstration that what a robot learns in our simulated worlds carries over to the real one. A model trained only on trajectories from simulation closed a dishwasher zero-shot in a live customer kitchen. We could not get this task working before, even with significant fine-tuning and teleoperated data collection.

      Virtual world from photographs

      With the advancements in the real2sim pipeline, our orchestrator agents improving the physics and texture and our team of 3d designers and physics engineers, we were able to create the environment where robots can learn and transfer the learnings in the real world.

      Learning only inside the worlds

      The robot then learned from trajectories it generated in those simulated worlds. For closing the dishwasher, we collected no real-world data at all: no demonstrations, no teleoperation, no fine-tuning on the customer's site.

      Zero real-world data for this task.

      Then we ran the model on the real robot in the real kitchen. It closed the dishwasher. The skill it had learned in our virtual kitchens transferred directly to the physical one.

      The robot learned to drop the plate

      The dishwasher was not the only thing the model figured out. In simulation, a plate got stuck at the edge of the rack, in a pose the gripper couldn't grasp well. Instead of retrying the same grasp, the model pushed the plate down, let it slide, and picked it up again from a better position.

      Dropping something on purpose so you can pick it up better is the kind of move people make without thinking, and the kind nobody writes into a dataset.

      This discovery is not alone

      There were more than 300 techniques that we traced back to sim explorations. This behaviour was not present in any data.

      This loop is super useful. The virtual world proposes endless possibilities on model finding techniques to navigate the real world. Each discovery becomes something the model can build on, which is what we mean by recursive learning.
